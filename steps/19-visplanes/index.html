Part 4 · Step 19

Visplanes and lighting

The last two pieces of Doom's renderer. Floors and ceilings are collected while the walls are drawn, then painted a row at a time, the fast direction from step 10. And light becomes a property of each sector, fading with distance, done entirely with a lookup table into 256 colours.

Floors want rows, walls want columns

Since step 17 we've drawn floors down each column, with a division for every pixel, because the columns are where the BSP walk is. But a floor is cheapest drawn along a row: every pixel of a row is at the same depth (step 10), so a row needs one division and then two additions per pixel. The trouble is that the walk produces floor in column-sized pieces, cut off at different heights by the walls and steps in front. We can't draw a whole row of floor; only the parts of rows that are actually floor, and actually this floor.

Doom's solution is to not draw floors during the walk at all, but to write down where they go. When a seg is drawn, the rows of each column above it (ceiling) and below it (floor) are recorded in a visplane: a record of one flat surface, identified by its height, texture and light level, with a top and bottom row for each screen column it covers.

// in drawSeg, for each column x, instead of drawing:
markPlane(r.ceilPlane, x, top[x], ceilY);      // ceiling rows above the wall
markPlane(r.floorPlane, x, floorY, bottom[x]); // floor rows below it

Every subsector looks up its floor's visplane, and its ceiling's, before drawing its segs: findPlane(height, texture, light) returns an existing visplane with the same three values if there is one, so the whole floor of a room is usually one visplane, even if it's seen in many pieces, and two different rooms with identical floors share one too. Only a floor below the eye, or a ceiling above it, can be seen at all.

A visplane holds one run of rows per column, though. If the same floor shows up again in a column it already has, for example beyond a low wall that hides the middle of it, the second piece goes into a new visplane with the same values. checkPlane handles that, for each seg, before marking anything.

From columns to spans

After the walk, every visplane is turned into horizontal spans. Go through its columns left to right, and compare the rows of column x − 1 with those of column x:

  • a row that's in the previous column but not this one is the end of a span: draw it, from where it started up to x − 1;
  • a row that's in this column but not the previous one is the start of a span: remember spanStart[y] = x.
function makeSpans(x, t1, b1, t2, b2) {   // rows t1…b1 at x − 1, t2…b2 at x
  while (t1 < t2 && t1 <= b1) { mapPlane(t1, spanStart[t1], x - 1); t1++; }   // ended at the top
  while (b1 > b2 && b1 >= t1) { mapPlane(b1, spanStart[b1], x - 1); b1--; }   // ended at the bottom
  while (t2 < t1 && t2 <= b2) { spanStart[t2] = x; t2++; }                   // started at the top
  while (b2 > b1 && b2 >= t2) { spanStart[b2] = x; b2--; }                   // started at the bottom
}

Since the rows of a visplane's column are always one unbroken run, the only rows that change between two columns are at the top and the bottom of the run, which is why four short loops cover every case. The visplane has an empty column on either side, so every span gets closed. Then mapPlane draws one span, and it's step 10's row loop with one change: the row's distance now depends on how far the surface is above or below the eye.

rowDist = |eyeZ − height| · projScale / |y + 0.5 − horizon|

Light

Doom's sectors each have a light level from 0 to 255. On top of that, light fades with distance, so a dark room's far end disappears into gloom while the walls next to you are still clear. In a bright room, light 255, nothing fades at all. This is the famous Doom atmosphere, and it costs almost nothing, thanks to two old tricks.

256 colours and a COLORMAP

Doom ran in VGA mode 13h, where each pixel is one byte: not a colour, but an index into a palette of 256 colours. Textures are stored the same way, as palette indices. Darkening a texel can't be done by multiplying, because there's no colour to multiply, only an index. Instead there's a table, the COLORMAP. It has 32 rows, one per light level from full bright (0) to black (31), and each row lists, for every palette index, the index of the palette colour closest to that colour darkened:

pixel = COLORMAP[level * 256 + texel];   // lighting, in one lookup

Our textures are still drawn in true colour by code. At startup they're reduced to 256 colours, with a palette chosen by median cut: put all the texture colours in one box, repeatedly cut the box with the widest spread of red, green or blue in half at the median, and stop at 255 boxes. Each box's average colour is a palette entry (plus black). Darkened copies of every colour go into the boxes too, so the palette has dark shades for the COLORMAP's darker rows to land on.

Which row of the COLORMAP?

The level for a wall column or a floor row comes from two things: the sector's light, and the depth. Doom's formula, in our units, is

level = (15 − light/16) · 4 − 20 / depth     clamped to 0 … 31

The first part is how dark the sector is far away: 0 (full bright) for light 255, beyond 31 for dark sectors. The second makes things brighter as they get nearer, by 20 levels at a depth of 1 and fading off as 1/depth. Depth is constant down a wall column and along a floor row, so the light level is too: one table row per column or per span, the same reason step 14's light falloff was cheap.

Walls get one more adjustment, Doom's version of step 6's darker walls: a wall running exactly east–west is one light step (16) darker than its sector, one running exactly north–south is one step brighter, and slanted walls get neither. It's called fake contrast, and it's why Doom's corners look sharp even in flat light.

The sky

The courtyard has no ceiling: its ceiling texture is the sky. The sky isn't a surface at any height; it's a picture infinitely far away. Its columns are chosen by the direction you're looking (the picture repeats four times around the horizon), its rows by the screen row, and it's always full bright. When a ceiling is sky, its visplane is drawn this way instead of as spans.

  • Show the visplanes. Each flat colour is one visplane. The whole start-room floor is one, even where the pillar cuts it in two. In the courtyard, the floor in front of the platform and the floor behind it share columns, so the same floor needs two visplanes.
  • Walk down to the crypt and watch the far wall breathe as its light swings between 96 and 176.
  • Turn the start room's light down to about 80 and look along the room: it fades to black in the distance, while the courtyard through the window stays bright.
  • Compare with exact colours, which dims the true colour instead of looking up the palette. In the dark the COLORMAP version is grainier and slightly off in hue. That's the 256-colour look.
  • Turn off the fading: each sector is lit evenly, and the level looks much flatter.

Visplanes, COLORMAP and PLAYPAL

Doom's visplanes, R_FindPlane, R_CheckPlane and R_MakeSpans, are what you've just seen. The original engine had room for only 128 visplanes per frame. A complex enough view, with many floors of different heights or light levels, ran out, and the game quit with “R_FindPlane: no more visplanes”. Mappers learned to stay under the limit; later source ports raised it or removed it.

Spans are drawn by R_DrawSpan from 64 × 64 textures called flats, with the texture position stepped in fixed point: step 10's loop, with an assembly-friendly packing of the two coordinates. The light level is picked from precomputed tables (scalelight for walls, indexed by on-screen scale, zlight for floors, indexed by distance), which is where our formula comes from. The flash of your own gun adds an extralight that brightens everything for a moment.

The COLORMAP lump has 34 tables: the 32 light levels, one inverted greyscale map for the invulnerability power-up, and one all black. The palette lump, PLAYPAL, holds 14 palettes. Only the first is the normal one; the others are tinted red for pain, gold for picking things up and green for the radiation suit. That's step 14's palette flash: switching to one of them recolours the whole screen at once.

A sector whose ceiling texture is F_SKY1 shows the sky, a 256-pixel-wide texture drawn by view angle at full brightness, which doesn't move when you go up or down stairs. Light specials on a sector, such as flickering, strobing and glowing, just change its light level every few tics, exactly like our crypt.

What we have

That's Part 4, and the end of the series. From a grid of cubes we went to a level drawn as polygons; walls projected from their two ends, with perspective-correct textures; sectors with their own floors, ceilings and light; a BSP tree that hands us the walls in the right order, so every pixel is drawn exactly once; floors drawn as spans; and lighting done with a table. Those are the core ideas of Doom's renderer, in under a thousand lines of engine code, comments included.

Some of Doom is still missing: sprites (things are drawn after the walls, far to near, clipped against the walls in front of them, much like step 11), moving floors and ceilings for doors and lifts (only the heights change, so the BSP tree survives), and transparent middle textures such as bars and fences. Each of these builds on pieces you've now seen.

After Doom, Quake (1996) went fully 3D: rooms above rooms, looking up and down, polygons at any angle. It kept the BSP tree, added precomputed visibility between its leaves, and divided for perspective every 16 pixels. A few years later, 3D graphics cards took over the pixel work, and the problems this series solved by hand moved into hardware. The ideas didn't go away: camera matrices, clipping, perspective-correct interpolation and front-to-back drawing still run on every GPU, millions of times per frame.
