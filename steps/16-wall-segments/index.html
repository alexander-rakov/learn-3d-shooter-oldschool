Part 4 · Step 16

Projecting wall segments

Instead of asking, for every screen column, which wall it sees, ask, for every wall, which columns it covers. Transform its two ends into camera space, cut off what's behind us, project, and fill in the columns between. The one subtle part is the texture, because depth doesn't change evenly across the screen. But one over depth does.

A wall is two points

A wall is a straight segment between two vertices. In 3D it's a rectangle standing on the floor, but every point of it projects the way its bottom edge does, so the segment is all we need. Step 11 placed a sprite on screen by moving one point into camera space. A wall is the same thing done for both ends:

const a = toCameraSpace(p, line.v1.x, line.v1.y);   // (u, v): v = depth, u/v = c
const b = toCameraSpace(p, line.v2.x, line.v2.y);
const x1 = (W / 2) * (1 + a.u / a.v);                 // screen columns of the two ends
const x2 = (W / 2) * (1 + b.u / b.v);

A straight line in the world projects to a straight line on screen, so the wall covers exactly the columns from x1 to x2. Projecting two points replaces 320 ray tests per wall, and walls we can't see cost almost nothing, because we find out early. There are three ways to be invisible:

  • Facing away. A one-sided wall is only ever seen from the front, so if pointOnSide (step 15) puts the camera behind it, we skip it. From anywhere in the level, the far side of every pillar and many walls of other rooms face away. This is back-face culling. As a bonus, a wall seen from the front always has x1 < x2: v1 is on the left.
  • Behind us. Both ends have v ≤ 0.
  • Off to the side. x2 ≤ 0 or x1 ≥ W.

Clipping at the near plane

The interesting case is a wall that runs from in front of us to behind us, like the wall beside you in a corridor. The end behind us has a negative v, and dividing by it flips it to the wrong side of the screen. An end with v close to 0 lands at an enormous x. So before projecting, we cut the wall at a near plane, a line just in front of the eye at v = NEAR_PLANE (0.05), and keep only the part beyond it.

Where does the wall cross the near plane? Moving along a straight wall, u and v both change linearly (camera space is the world, moved and turned). So the crossing is the fraction

f = (NEAR − v1) / (v2 − v1)

of the way from v1 to v2, and the new end is u1 + f·(u2 − u1), v = NEAR. The same f moves the wall's texture coordinate, which we'll need in a moment. This is how every 3D renderer since has handled “behind the camera”: clip first, then divide.

Filling in the columns, correctly

Between x1 and x2, each column needs two things: the wall's depth there, for its height, and how far along the wall it is, for the texture column. We know both at the two ends. It's tempting to blend linearly between them across the screen, but that's wrong. Equal steps on screen are not equal steps along the wall. The near half of a wall covers more of the screen than the far half.

The fix is a small piece of algebra. The wall lies on a straight line in camera space, which can be written A·u + B·v = D for some numbers A, B and D. Divide by v, and remember that u/v is the screen position c:

A·c + B = D / v   ⇒   1/v = (A·c + B) / D

So 1/v, one over depth, is a linear function of the screen position. It changes by the same amount from column to column. The distance along the wall, call it along, is linear in u and v, so the same division shows that along/v is linear in c too. The recipe:

let iz = 1 / v1, az = along1 / v1;                       // at x1
const dIz = (1 / v2 - iz) / (x2 - x1);                    // constant steps per column
const dAz = (along2 / v2 - az) / (x2 - x1);
for (let x = xStart; x < xEnd; x++, iz += dIz, az += dAz) {
  const depth = 1 / iz;
  const along = az * depth;                              // divide out the 1/v again
  const lineHeight = projScale * iz;                     // = projScale / depth, no division
  // … draw the column exactly as in step 15
}

Two additions and one division per column. This is called perspective-correct interpolation, and every GPU does it today, for every pixel of every triangle. The column's lineHeight doesn't even need the division: it's projScale / depth, so it's linear across the screen as well. A wall's top and bottom edges on screen are straight lines.

Tick affine texture in the demo to interpolate along directly instead. Make the wall long and turn it away from you: the bricks bunch up at the near end and stretch at the far one, and they swim as the wall moves. Early 3D hardware such as the original PlayStation did exactly this, which is why its textures wobble.

Which wall wins a column?

Ray casting answered that for free: the first wall the ray hits. Now walls are drawn one at a time, in whatever order the map lists them, and several can cover the same column. For now we use the one-row depth buffer from step 11. Each column remembers the depth of the wall drawn there, and another wall only draws over it if it's nearer:

if (depth >= zBuffer[x]) continue;   // something nearer is already here
zBuffer[x] = depth;

Every wall spans the full height of its column (every floor is at 0 and every ceiling at 1), so one depth per column is enough, as it was for sprites.

  • Turn off the depth test. Walls are drawn in map order, and wherever a far wall comes later in the list than a near one, it's painted over it.
  • Watch the counts as you turn: around 40% of the walls face away from wherever you stand.
  • Stand in the hall and look towards the blue room. Every wall in the room beyond the corridor is drawn, then mostly painted over by nearer walls. The readout's “× the wall area” number goes up. That wasted work grows with the size of the level. Step 18 fixes it.
  • Hug a long wall and look along it: it's clipped at the near plane every frame.

Angles instead of a near plane

Doom does this with angles. R_AddLine turns each end of a wall into the angle at which the viewer sees it (R_PointToAngle, a table lookup on the slope), and clips the pair of angles to the 90° field of view. Any part of the wall outside it, including anything behind the viewer, is simply gone, and the clipped angles go through a table, viewangletox, to become screen columns. Walls facing away come out with their angles the wrong way round, and are dropped like ours.

The column loop, R_RenderSegLoop, steps rw_scale, the wall's on-screen scale, by a constant rw_scalestep per column. Scale is projection / depth, so that's exactly our 1/v stepping. The texture column is worked out per column from the angle again: with rw_distance the perpendicular distance from the eye to the wall's line, it's rw_offset − tan(angle) · rw_distance, which is exact rather than interpolated. The scale is clamped to a maximum, so a wall right in your face doesn't get an absurd height.

The reason none of this needs a division per pixel is Doom's big simplification: walls are always vertical, and floors are always horizontal, and you can't look up or down. Every wall column is at one depth, and (step 19) every floor row is too. Quake (1996), with true 3D, had to divide for perspective along its spans, and did it only every 16 pixels, interpolating in between. The PlayStation's texture wobble comes from not doing it at all.

And Doom has no depth buffer. It draws walls in an order where nothing drawn later can be in front of anything drawn earlier, so a column can be marked “done” and never touched again. Finding that order is what step 18 is about.

Next

So far every floor is at 0 and every ceiling at 1, so two-sided lines are invisible. Doom's sectors each have their own floor and ceiling height. Then a two-sided line isn't invisible any more: it's a step, a ledge, or a window, with wall above and below it. That breaks the depth buffer, since a column can now show several walls stacked on top of each other.
