Part 1 · Step 3

The camera: direction, screen plane and camera space

A raycaster shoots one ray per column of pixels. This step works out exactly which direction each of those rays goes, and builds the 2 × 2 matrix that converts between world coordinates and the camera's own coordinates. That matrix comes back for floors (step 10) and sprites (Part 3).

Two vectors make a camera

The camera is described by three things:

  • pos: where the eye is.
  • d: the direction it looks in, a unit vector.
  • p: the camera plane, a vector perpendicular to d pointing to the viewer's right: p = perp(d) · tan(FOV/2).

Picture the screen as a flat window held one unit in front of the eye. Its centre is at pos + d; its left edge is at pos + d − p and its right edge at pos + d + p. Every pixel column of the screen is a point on that window, and the ray for that column goes from the eye through it.

If the screen is W pixels wide, column x sits at a position c between −1 (left edge) and +1 (right edge):

c = 2·x / W − 1     rayDir(x) = d + c·p

The field of view follows from the triangle formed by the eye, the window centre and the window edge: the half-angle has tangent |p| / |d| = |p|. So |p| = tan(FOV/2). A 66° FOV gives |p| ≈ 0.65; 90° gives exactly 1.

// One ray per screen column
for (let x = 0; x < W; x++) {
  const c = 2 * x / W - 1;              // -1 … +1 across the screen
  const rayDirX = dirX + planeX * c;
  const rayDirY = dirY + planeY * c;
  // … cast this ray (steps 4–5) and draw a wall column (step 6)
}

Rays are evenly spaced on the plane, not in angle

Note what we did not do: divide the FOV into W equal angles. The screen is flat, so equal pixel steps are equal steps along the plane, and the angles between neighbouring rays get smaller towards the edges. Use equal angles and straight walls come out bent; step 7 shows this.

Turning the camera

To turn by α, rotate both vectors with the rotation matrix from step 2. Since rotation preserves lengths and right angles, p stays perpendicular to d and the FOV stays the same:

[dirX,   dirY]   = rotate(dirX, dirY, α)
[planeX, planeY] = rotate(planeX, planeY, α)

This is why many raycasters (including ours) store the camera as two vectors instead of an angle: no trigonometry is needed per frame, just one rotation when you press a key.

Camera space

The other direction is just as important: given a point q in the world (an enemy, a lamp, the corner of a wall), where does it appear on screen?

The trick is to describe q in the camera's own coordinates. Use p and d as basis vectors and find u, v such that

q − pos = u·p + v·d

  • v is the depth: how far in front of the camera the point is. Negative means behind.
  • u is how far to the side it is, in units of p.

Written as a matrix, the two basis vectors become the columns of the camera matrix M:

q − pos = M · (u, v)T,    M = [ px   dx ;   py   dy ]

M takes camera coordinates to world coordinates. To go the other way we invert it. For a 2 × 2 matrix the inverse is: swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant.

(u, v)T = M−1 · (q − pos),    M−1 = 1/det · [ dy   −dx ;   −py   px ],    det = pxdy − dxpy

const rx = qx - posX, ry = qy - posY;            // q relative to the eye
const invDet = 1 / (planeX * dirY - dirX * planeY);
const u = invDet * ( dirY   * rx - dirX   * ry); // sideways, in units of p
const v = invDet * (-planeY * rx + planeX * ry); // depth

(Since p ⟂ d and |d| = 1, this simplifies to v = (q − pos)·d and u = (q − pos)·p / |p|², which are dot products, as in step 1. The general inverse is worth knowing because it still works if you ever skew the camera.)

Perspective in one division

Now the projection. The ray through column c is d + c·p. In camera coordinates that's the set of points where u / v = c. So a point with camera coordinates (u, v) lands on

c = u / v    ⇒    screenX = (W/2) · (1 + u / v)

Dividing by depth is what perspective is: things twice as far away shift half as much. Step 6 applies the same division to wall heights.

  • Move the target behind the eye: v goes negative and the point disappears from the screen. Without a v > 0 check, sprites behind you would show up mirrored.
  • Move the target straight away from the eye along one ray. u and v both grow, but u / v (and the screen column) stays put.
  • Widen the FOV towards 140° and watch how unevenly the rays fan out in angle, even though they're evenly spaced along the green plane.

Per-column angle tables and a shrinkable screen

Wolfenstein 3D tracks the player by angle rather than by vectors, so it needs the angle of each column's ray relative to the view direction. That's atan(c · tan(FOV/2)), the same plane-spaced rays as ours. It precomputes these once into a table called pixelangle[] (in CalcProjection), so at runtime each column's ray angle is just viewangle + pixelangle[x].

The view width wasn't fixed either. The options menu had a “Change View” setting that shrank the 3D window (with a border around it) so slow PCs had fewer columns to cast and draw. Changing it rebuilt the projection tables.
