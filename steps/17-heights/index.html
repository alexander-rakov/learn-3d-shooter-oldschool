Part 4 · Step 17

Variable heights: steps, windows and portals

Give every sector its own floor and ceiling height, and a two-sided line stops being invisible. It becomes a step, a ledge or a window, with wall above and below an opening. A column of the screen can now show several things stacked up, and the order we draw in starts to matter.

Heights

A sector now has a floor and a ceil. Our level has stairs down into a crypt, a raised window between two rooms, and a sunken courtyard with a platform in it. The eye is EYE_HEIGHT = 0.5 above the floor you're standing on, at height eyeZ.

Projecting heights is step 6 again. At depth v, one map unit is scale = projScale / v pixels tall, and the horizon is the screen's middle row. A point at height z appears

y = horizon − (z − eyeZ) · scale

That's the formula behind every wall so far, with z = 1 for the top and z = 0 for the bottom and the eye at 0.5. Now z is whatever the sector says. Since scale is projScale · (1/v), and we already step 1/v across the wall's columns (step 16), each column costs a multiplication per height.

Upper and lower walls

Stand in one sector and look across a two-sided line into the next. The line itself has no wall. But if the sector beyond has a lower ceiling, you see the strip of wall between the two ceilings hanging down above the opening: the upper wall, like the lintel above a door. If it has a higher floor, you see a strip between the two floors: the lower wall, like a step. In between is the opening, and through it you see the next sector.

Which texture goes there? In Doom each sidedef has three: upper, lower and middle (for one-sided walls). Our sidedefs have one, and buildMap picks it with a simple rule. The step you see is the edge of the other sector's floor, and the lintel the edge of its ceiling, so a sidedef gets the other sector's wall texture.

Clip arrays

Step 16 kept one depth per column. That's no use now: a column can show a ceiling, a lintel, a far wall seen through a window, a step and a floor, all at different depths. Doom's answer, and ours, is two numbers per column, top[x] and bottom[x]: the rows that are still open. Everything nearer has been drawn above top and below bottom, and whatever comes next must fit in between.

Drawing one column of a seg goes like this:

const ceilY  = row where the front sector's ceiling meets this wall   // clamped to top … bottom
const floorY = row where the front sector's floor meets it
drawFlatColumn(x, top[x], ceilY, front.ceil);            // ceiling above the wall
drawFlatColumn(x, floorY, bottom[x], front.floor);       // floor below it
if (!back) {
  drawWallColumn(x, ceilY, floorY);                      // a solid wall fills the rest…
  top[x] = h; bottom[x] = 0;                             // …and closes the column
} else {
  if (back.ceil < front.ceil)  draw the upper wall from ceilY down to the back ceiling;
  if (back.floor > front.floor) draw the lower wall from the back floor down to floorY;
  top[x] = bottom of the upper wall (or ceilY);          // the opening is all that's left
  bottom[x] = top of the lower wall (or floorY);
}

The clip arrays only ever shrink. That works if, and only if, we draw near to far: once something is drawn, nothing drawn later can be in front of it. In return, every pixel is written exactly once. There's no overdraw at all, which step 16's depth buffer couldn't promise.

Floors and ceilings, column by column

A sector's floor is visible below each of its walls, from the wall's foot down to bottom[x]. So we draw it there, a column at a time, with step 10's idea: a row of a horizontal surface is at one depth. That depth now comes from the height difference between the eye and the floor:

rowDist = (eyeZ − z) · projScale / (y + 0.5 − horizon)

With z = 0 and eyeZ = 0.5 that's step 10's formula. For a ceiling, eyeZ − z is negative and so is the row's distance from the horizon, so the same line works. But going down a column instead of along a row, nothing is constant from one pixel to the next: it's a division per pixel. That's the price of drawing floors in the wrong direction, and step 19 shows how Doom avoids it.

Near to far: portals

Here is one way to get the near-to-far order. Start in the sector the player is standing in and draw all of its segs we can see. Each two-sided seg is a portal, a window into the next sector, covering columns a to b. After the sector's own walls are done, go through each portal and render the sector behind it, but only in the portal's columns. Anything you see through a window is farther away than the window, so the order is right, and the clip arrays keep it inside the opening:

function renderSector(sector, xl, xr, fromLine) {
  for (const seg of sector.segs) {
    if (seg.line === fromLine) continue;                 // the portal we came in through
    const proj = projectSeg(p, seg);                     // step 16: cull, clip, project
    if (proj.culled) continue;
    const a = max(xl, ceil(proj.x1)), b = min(xr, ceil(proj.x2));
    if (a < b) { drawSeg(seg, proj, a, b); if (seg.back) portals.push({ seg, a, b }); }
  }
  for (const { seg, a, b } of portals)                   // then what's behind each window
    if (any column from a to b is still open) renderSector(seg.back, a, b, seg.line);
}
renderSector(playerSector, 0, W, null);

One special case: standing in a doorway, the portal line can be closer than the near plane, and step 16's clipping would throw it away. Then it covers the whole view anyway, so we look straight through it.

  • Look at the window from across the start room: a lower wall below it (the sill), an upper wall above it, and the courtyard through the gap. The tree shows window → courtyard N.
  • Walk down the stairs. Each step is a sector, and its ceiling steps down too, so you see a row of lintels. Turn round at the bottom and look up: now the steps' lower walls face you.
  • Walk up to the window and try to climb through. The sill is 0.45 high, more than the 0.375 you can step up. The platform in the courtyard is 0.35 above its floor, so that one you can climb.
  • Tick the concave courtyard and look at the platform. It vanishes.

The catch: convex sectors only

Within one sector, segs are drawn in the order they're listed. Nothing sorts them. That's fine if no seg of a sector can ever hide another seg of the same sector, and that's exactly what being convex means: from any point inside, you can see every wall in full. Looking out from inside a convex room, its walls never overlap on screen.

The courtyard has a platform in the middle, so as a single sector it has a hole and isn't convex. From inside it, the platform's edges are in front of the walls behind them. With the checkbox ticked, the courtyard's outer walls come first in its list. They're drawn first, close their columns, and the platform, which is nearer, finds nothing open. In the unticked level the courtyard was split by hand into four convex sectors around the platform, with identical heights, so the lines between them are portals with no walls.

Splitting every room into convex pieces by hand is tedious, and level designers shouldn't have to think about it. That is the job of the next step's BSP tree: it does the splitting automatically, at build time, and it gives the drawing order for free.

Moving between heights

Collision gets a rule for two-sided lines. The opening between two sectors runs from the higher floor to the lower ceiling. You can pass if it's at least PLAYER_HEIGHT (0.7) tall and its bottom is at most MAX_STEP (0.375) above the floor you're standing on:

function blocksMove(line, floor) {
  if (!line.back) return true;
  const openTop = Math.min(a.ceil, b.ceil), openBottom = Math.max(a.floor, b.floor);
  return openTop - openBottom < PLAYER_HEIGHT || openBottom - floor > MAX_STEP;
}

Stepping down is always allowed. After each move sectorAt tells us which floor we're on, and the eye eases towards its new height instead of jumping, which turns stairs into a smooth glide.

ceilingclip, floorclip, and someone else's portals

Doom's clip arrays are called ceilingclip[] and floorclip[], one short integer per column, and R_RenderSegLoop narrows them exactly like our drawSeg. Each sidedef has three texture names, upper, lower and middle. Two flags on the linedef, upper unpegged and lower unpegged, choose how a texture is anchored: to the ceiling, or to the edge of the step. Mappers used them to make door tracks stay put while the door moves, and to keep a lift's side from sliding as it rises.

The numbers are Doom's own, scaled: a step of 24 units (our 0.375) is the most you can climb, the player is 56 units tall, and the eye is 41 units above the floor. When you walk up a step, Doom lifts the player at once but lowers the view by the same amount, then lets it catch up over a few frames, much like our easing.

Doom does not render through portals, though. That's the Build engine's way. Ken Silverman, who had written Ken's Labyrinth, a Wolfenstein-style game, went on to write Build, the engine of Duke Nukem 3D (1996). Build draws the player's sector, then the sectors visible through its two-sided walls (the red lines in its editor), recursively, much like our renderSector. It doesn't require convex sectors: it groups each sector's visible walls into bunches and works out at run time which bunch is in front. Portals also let Build do things a BSP can't: sectors can move and change shape while you play, and two rooms can occupy the same spot on the map, as long as you can never see both at once.

Next

Portal rendering needs convex sectors, or work at run time to sort walls within a sector. Doom took a third way: do all the sorting once, when the level is built, and store it in a tree.
