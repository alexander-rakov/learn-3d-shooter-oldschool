Part 2 · Step 7

The fisheye problem (and why the fix is free)

Last step's renderer bends straight walls. The cause is subtle: perspective divides by depth, not by distance. The fix turns out to be the t that DDA already gives us.

Distance is not depth

Recall from step 3 how a point projects: screenX = W/2 · (1 + u/v), where v is the point's depth, the coordinate along the view direction d. The projection plane is flat and perpendicular to d, so the similar triangles of step 6 are measured along d too. Height on screen is projScale / depth.

In step 6 we divided by the Euclidean distance instead. For the centre column they're the same. For a ray at angle φ off-centre, the Euclidean distance to a point is longer than its depth:

depth = distance · cos φ

So towards the screen edges we divided by too much, and the walls came out too short. On a straight wall facing you, the depth is the same for every column, so the wall should be a perfect rectangle. The Euclidean distance grows towards the edges, which produces the bulge.

Why the edges are straight

A nice fact falls out of this. Along a straight wall, depth changes linearly with position. So does the screen coordinate, and 1 / depth turns out to be linear in screen x. Since a column's height is projScale / depth, the top and bottom edges of any flat wall are straight lines on screen. That's what a pinhole camera does. A fisheye lens doesn't, and neither did our step-6 renderer.

The fix: DDA's t is already the depth

We could compute the angle φ for every column and multiply by cos φ. Many raycaster tutorials do exactly that. But look at the ray we built in step 3:

rayDir = d + c·p

The hit point is pos + t·rayDir. Its depth is its offset from the eye, dotted with d:

depth = (t·rayDir) · d = t·(d·d) + t·c·(p·d) = t·1 + t·c·0 = t

because d has length 1 and p is perpendicular to it. In other words, a ray built from the camera plane has a component of exactly 1 along d. The ray parameter t from DDA is the perpendicular distance. The fix is to delete the Math.hypot:

// step 6 (fisheye):
const dist = hit.t * Math.hypot(rayDirX, rayDirY);
// step 7 (correct): t already is the depth
const dist = hit.t;

That's one less square root per column, and it's also the reason for not normalising the ray direction. This step's raycaster.js keeps both versions behind an option so you can compare them; it computes the depth as t · (rayDir · d), which is just t for plane rays.

The other half: angle-spaced rays

Step 3 claimed rays should be spaced evenly on the plane, not evenly in angle. Here's why. Each column's height is computed correctly from its own ray, but if the rays aren't where that column actually looks, everything gets squeezed sideways. A flat screen gives each degree near the edges more columns than each degree in the middle (the plane is further from the eye out there). Equal angles give every degree the same number of columns, so compared to a true projection the middle is stretched and the sides are compressed. Straight lines bend again, more gently than the fisheye but visibly at wide FOVs.

  • Euclidean distance, 66° FOV, facing the long wall at the end of the starting corridor: it bulges. Switch to perpendicular: flat.
  • Perpendicular distance, “even in angle”, FOV 130°: walk along a wall and watch its edge bend near the screen sides.
  • Push the FOV to 140° with everything correct: no bending, but extreme stretching at the edges. That's real perspective at a wide angle; wide-angle photos look the same.

Wolfenstein's depth, and its odd FOV

Wolfenstein traces by angle (step 3's pixelangle[]), so it doesn't get depth for free the way plane rays do. Instead, after finding a hit, its CalcHeight routine projects the hit point's offset onto the view direction: a dot product with the view's cosine and sine, in fixed point. That's the same perpendicular depth, computed explicitly.

Because the per-column angles come from atan of evenly spaced plane positions, its rays are plane-spaced as well, so straight walls stay straight.

One thing we ignore: 320 × 200 was shown on a 4:3 monitor, so real VGA pixels were about 20% taller than wide. Art of that era was drawn with that stretch in mind; on today's square pixels it all looks slightly squashed.
