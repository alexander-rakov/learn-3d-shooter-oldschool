An interactive series

Building an old-school 3D shooter, from the maths up

How did Wolfenstein 3D draw a 3D world on a 1992 PC with no floating point and no graphics card? This series rebuilds the technique step by step in plain JavaScript, with an interactive demo on every page. Everything runs in the browser, with no libraries and no build step.

Each step lives in its own folder with a complete, runnable copy of the code at that point. From step 5 on, the engine is in a file called raycaster.js. Diff it against the previous step's copy to see exactly what changed. Boxes titled “How it was done in 1992” compare our version with what the original games did.

Why this exists

I wanted to build something like this a long time ago, back in school, around 6th or 7th grade. I didn't know the maths yet (we hadn't even got to trigonometry), and afterwards I never had the chance to think the idea through from start to finish. The closest I came was a second-year university course where our labs had us draw graphs by writing directly to video memory.

This series is me finally doing it properly, step by step, learning the maths along the way. I'm doing it together with an AI, and it has been a surprisingly fun and productive way to learn: a teacher and study buddy who is competent, patient, and available around the clock. What a great time to learn new things!

What surprised me along the way

  1. Fixed-point numbers. We've had hardware floating point for so long that storing fractions as plain integers (16 bits for the whole part, 16 for the fraction) feels like a stroke of genius today. Then again, it's the same trick we still use for money: store cents as integers and never trust a float with a balance. The only difference is that 16.16 counts in 1/65536ths instead of hundredths. I was surprised anyway.
  2. Darker N/S walls. Drawing one side of every wall at half brightness looks trivial today, but it adds so much of a 3D feel for almost no cost: one if and a bit shift per column. Unbelievable.
  3. Euclidean distance bends straight lines. Photography is my hobby, so I should have seen this coming: it's the difference between a fisheye and a rectilinear lens. Still, it was a surprise that the fix is to account for the flat screen, by dividing by depth rather than by the real distance to the wall.

Part 1: The maths

All top-down, all 2D. No 3D picture yet, just the tools.

  1. Vectors and coordinates: points, vectors, the dot product, perpendiculars. 1992: fixed-point numbers.
  2. Angles, sine, cosine and rotation: the rotation matrix. 1992: sine tables and binary angles.
  3. The camera: direction + screen plane, one ray per column, camera space and its matrix.
  4. Ray marching: the naive way to find a wall, and why it's too slow and inaccurate.
  5. DDA grid traversal: exact ray casting with nothing but additions.

Part 2: From 2D to “3D”

  1. Perspective projection: similar triangles, our first rendered frame.
  2. The fisheye problem: distance vs. depth, and why DDA's t is the fix.
  3. Movement and collision: frame-rate independence, strafing, sliding along walls.
  4. Textured walls: procedural embossed bricks, texture coordinates.
  5. Floor and ceiling casting: the camera matrix run forwards.

Part 3: Making it a game

  1. Sprites: objects and enemies, camera-space transform, depth buffer
  2. Doors and push-walls
  3. Shooting and enemies: hitscan, line of sight, a tiny state machine
  4. HUD, weapon, pickups: a playable level

Part 4: How Doom did it differently

  1. From grids to polygons: vertices, linedefs, sectors
  2. Projecting wall segments and clipping
  3. Variable floor and ceiling heights
  4. BSP trees: front-to-back without sorting
  5. Visplanes and lighting

Controls in the game demos: click the view first, then ↑ ↓ / W S to move, ← → to turn, A D to strafe (from step 8), Shift to run.
